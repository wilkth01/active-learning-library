The idea

Put students in the position the reading is about, make them feel the tensions.

Discussions too often fail in just the same way. The case does the moral work, everyone agrees in thirty seconds, and the next forty minutes are spent with everyone (the instructor included) struggling to figure out what to say short of introducing a new case. The fix is rarely a better question (though sometimes it is). It’s usually a better structure: something students have to decide, sort, rule on or defend before the theory arrives. They feel the tension, and they have to use the theory to wrestle with it.

In Fall 2026, I used Claude to help build that structure in my Business Ethics, Ethics, and Nursing Science I courses. As I planned out each week’s learning goals and content, I worked with Claude to brainstorm active learning exercises that simulate the circumstances to which the week’s theoretical apparatus responds. As the semester progressed, I started noticing patterns in the kinds of exercises that worked. They’re variations on themes:

  • Decide, then reveal. Students make an ordinary choice with ordinary criteria, commit to it, and then learn what the choice did that nobody intended.
  • Run the rule. Students apply a test exactly as written, find what it misses, and then run it on a case nobody would accept.
  • Sort, score, build. Students put the reading’s distinctions to work with their hands: piles, scores, cages.
  • Roles and verdicts. Students argue from a seat, a theory or a side they didn’t choose, and the argument has to end in a ruling.
  • Discussion structures. Named moves and watched circles that turn talk into argument.
  • Oral assessment. A position, the best objection, a new fact: reasoning out loud is what gets graded.
  • Small moves. Five-minute techniques that open, close or turn a session.

So I filed them by structure instead of by week. Each format here names the slots you swap out (the case, the cards, the reveal) and the ways it goes wrong, so you can lift it into a different course without inheriting my readings.

Brainstorm

Try a format on your own topic.

Every format page has a small brainstorm panel. Tell it your course and your topic, and Claude sketches three versions of that format for your room. Pick one and it writes the run of show.

  1. Describe your class. Course, topic or reading, minutes, class size. A sentence of constraints if you have them.
  2. Get three directions. Each one names the case, the turn where minds change, and the most likely way it goes wrong.
  3. Develop the one you like. A timed run of show with the cards, prompts and debrief written out.

It’s deliberately small: a few requests per format, and it only does this one job. For a longer back-and-forth, every panel can hand the same prompt to Claude directly.

Notes for a colleague

What held across all twenty-six.

Make them commit before you reveal anything.

The single most reliable move in the library. A group’s choice and its one deciding reason go on the board in its own words before the new fact appears. Without the commitment, students rewrite their reasons after the fact and the reveal teaches nothing.

Give the role a real goal.

If the siting team isn’t trying to save money, students hunt for the trick instead of doing the job. The mechanism only shows up when people pursue ordinary ends in good faith.

The trick spends after one use.

After the first reveal, tell students the trap up front and ask them to beat it. The second version teaches something the first couldn’t: how hard the trap is to avoid even when you see it.

Ban the easy word.

“Greed,” “intentions,” “intersectionality.” Each one lets students skip the argument it names. Lift the ban once the strongest defense is on paper.

Hold the line on “we can’t tell.”

Give every sort a pile for what the text doesn’t establish, and every vote a third option. Claiming only what the source supports is a skill, and it has to be practiced.

Leave with a tool, not a summary.

A four-question check built across a week is worth more than a recap slide. Students reuse it on new cases, and later in oral exams.

Don’t let the reveal become a verdict on the students.

Most careful people make the same choice. The debrief is about the structure that made the choice reasonable, not about who should have known better.
